Bulk RNA-seq Deconvolution in Tumor Samples

How deconvolution estimates immune, stromal, and malignant cell composition from bulk tumor RNA-seq, which tools answer which question, and where the estimates can mislead.

Kiran Adhikari June 13, 2026 11 min read

Bulk RNA-seq deconvolution is a computational method for estimating which cell types are present in a mixed tissue sample, and in what relative amounts, from its combined gene expression profile.

In cancer research, it is mainly used to characterize the tumor microenvironment (TME): immune-cell infiltration, stromal composition, and, with appropriate reference data, malignant-cell populations.

The key point is that a tumor biopsy does not contain only cancer cells. It can contain malignant cells, T cells, B cells, macrophages, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and other populations. Standard bulk RNA-seq measures the combined RNA from all of them. Deconvolution tries to separate their contributions computationally.

1. What does deconvolution actually do?

When you sequence a whole tumor sample, you get one gene expression profile, even though the tissue contains many cell types.

The algorithm compares the observed expression with reference expression patterns for individual cell types. For example, signatures that include CD3D, CD3E, and TRAC help identify T-cell contributions, while MS4A1, CD79A, and CD79B support B-cell identification. Each population needs a multigene signature, not a single marker.

A simplified version of the model is:

\[ Y_{g,s} \approx \sum_{k=1}^{K} S_{g,k}\, W_{k,s} + \epsilon_{g,s} \]

where:

  • \(Y_{g,s}\) is the observed bulk expression of gene \(g\) in sample \(s\)
  • \(S_{g,k}\) is the reference expression signature of cell type \(k\)
  • \(W_{k,s}\) is the estimated contribution of cell type \(k\) to sample \(s\)
  • \(\epsilon_{g,s}\) is unexplained expression and model error

Depending on the method, \(W\) is an RNA contribution, a normalized cell fraction, or an enrichment score. These quantities are not interchangeable. A cell-type RNA fraction is not automatically the true percentage of cells in the tissue.

This framework underlies established methods such as CIBERSORT and CIBERSORTx [1, 2].

2. Why use deconvolution in tumor research?

The main purpose is to separate changes in tumor-cell biology from changes in the cellular composition of the tissue.

Consider two tumors:

  • Tumor A: high malignant-cell content, few infiltrating immune cells
  • Tumor B: lower malignant-cell content, substantial CD8 T-cell and macrophage infiltration

Both produce bulk RNA-seq data, but the expression differences between them may be driven by their different cell populations rather than by transcriptional regulation inside the cancer cells. This is a major source of confounding in tumor differential expression analysis.

These applications are supported by studies using CIBERSORTx, quanTIseq, EPIC, and BayesPrism [2–4, 9]. They produce estimates suitable for research analysis, not direct observations of each cell.

3. Which tools are available?

Different tools answer different questions, and they should not be treated as interchangeable.

ToolMain purposeOutputBest use in tumor studies
CIBERSORT / CIBERSORTx [1, 2]Resolve immune subpopulations using signature matricesRelative fractions; CIBERSORTx also offers absolute-mode scores and inferred cell-type expressionDetailed immune profiling
EPIC [3]Quantify immune and stromal cells, with an uncharacterized compartmentEstimated cell fractionsOverall TME composition
quanTIseq [4]Quantify ten immune cell typesFractions relative to all cellsTumor immune infiltration
MCP-counter [5]Abundance of immune and stromal populationsScores in arbitrary unitsComparing infiltration between samples
xCell [6]Enrichment of many immune and stromal cell typesEnrichment scoresBroad immune and stromal screening
TIMER [7]Selected immune populations with cancer-type-aware signaturesInfiltration estimatesTumor immune association studies
BayesPrism [9]Jointly infer composition and cell-type-specific expression from single-cell referencesRNA-contribution fractions and inferred expressionTumor heterogeneity and tumor–TME interactions

Two distinctions matter most:

  1. CIBERSORT relative fractions are not percentages of all tumor cells. With the LM22 reference, fractions are normalized across the 22 modeled leukocyte populations only.
  2. MCP-counter and xCell scores are not cell percentages. MCP-counter is designed to compare the same cell population across samples, and xCell returns enrichment scores. Their values cannot be compared directly across different cell types.

How relative fractions can mislead

Suppose two biopsies have these independently measured compositions:

MeasureTumor ATumor B
Immune cells among all tissue cells2%20%
CD8 T cells among immune cells50%25%
CD8 T cells among all tissue cells1%5%

A relative immune-cell deconvolution could report 50% CD8 T cells for Tumor A and 25% for Tumor B. But Tumor B actually has five times the proportion of CD8 T cells across all of its cells. Concluding that Tumor A has more CD8 T-cell infiltration from relative LM22 fractions alone would be wrong.

The distinction between within-sample and between-sample comparisons is discussed in detail in the benchmark by Sturm et al. [8], and it is why methods such as EPIC and quanTIseq estimate fractions relative to all cells, including an uncharacterized compartment [3, 4].

4. What does the output look like?

For a cohort, the main output is usually a matrix with one row per sample and one column per cell population.

Hypothetical EPIC-like output, for illustration only:

SampleCD8 TCD4 TB cellsMacrophagesOther cells
Tumor_010.080.050.030.090.75
Tumor_020.190.110.080.120.50
Tumor_030.020.030.010.040.90
Tumor_040.140.070.090.150.55

The “other cells” column can include malignant cells and any population not resolved separately. It must not automatically be labeled as tumor purity.

Other common outputs include immune-cell heatmaps, tumor-versus-normal comparisons, correlations between cell populations, treatment-associated changes, survival models, and cell-type-specific expression profiles when the algorithm supports them.

Reference batch effectsscRNA-seq and bulk RNA-seq measure expression differentlySystematically biased estimates
No spatial informationHomogenization destroys tissue structureCannot tell tumor-infiltrating cells from peripheral or TLS-localized ones

The benchmark by Sturm et al. (2019) showed that accuracy varies substantially by cell type and reference signature. Methods do better for transcriptionally distinct populations than for closely related immune subsets [8].

Also note that the CIBERSORTx empirical p-value measures whether the reference-based fit beats a random baseline. It is not a p-value for a difference in cell abundance between groups [2].

6. A practical workflow for tumor bulk RNA-seq

  1. FASTQ → QC → alignment / quantification
  2. Normalized expression matrix: non-log values, normalized as the chosen tool expects
  3. Reference selection + deconvolution: CIBERSORTx, EPIC, quanTIseq, or BayesPrism
  4. Composition matrix + QC: reference coverage, model fit, purity, and agreement across methods
  5. Statistical and biological analysis: group comparisons, survival, immune signatures, integration with DEGs

For CIBERSORTx, the published protocol uses TPM input and requires non-log, non-negative expression. Batch correction should match how the reference and mixture were generated; for example, signatures built from droplet-based scRNA-seq may need S-mode correction [10].

For a real tumor cohort, I would:

  1. Estimate immune and stromal abundance with EPIC or quanTIseq.
  2. Run CIBERSORTx with LM22 for finer immune subpopulations, or with a tumor-matched single-cell reference when one is available.
  3. Check that the inferred populations agree with marker expression, pathology, and independent estimates.
  4. Compare populations between groups with appropriate covariate adjustment and FDR control, using compositional methods when modeling fractions.
  5. Integrate with bulk DEGs and pathway analysis, explicitly separating infiltration-driven expression changes from cell-intrinsic regulation.

Deconvolution fractions should not automatically be added as covariates to every DE model. Whether to adjust depends on whether cell composition is a confounder or a biological consequence of the condition you are studying.

Bottom line

Deconvolution turns bulk tumor RNA-seq into estimates of cellular composition. Its main value is showing how immune and stromal infiltration varies across patients, treatments, and outcomes, and helping separate composition effects from tumor-cell-intrinsic transcriptional changes.

For general tumor immune profiling, CIBERSORTx plus EPIC or quanTIseq gives complementary information. For tumor-cell-specific transcriptional programs, a suitable tumor-matched single-cell reference with BayesPrism or CIBERSORTx expression imputation is more appropriate.
