RNA-seq Red Flags: What to Check Before You Trust Your Results

A stage-by-stage list of the RNA-seq QC signals that actually matter, what each usually means, and how to check it before it reaches your results.

Kiran Adhikari August 20, 2026 6 min read

Most RNA-seq problems are not hidden. They show up in the QC report, in the PCA plot, or in the p-value histogram. They just get skipped because the pipeline finished without errors and the volcano plot looks reasonable.

A finished pipeline is not a validated dataset. Below are the signals I check before trusting any result, grouped by the stage where they appear. None of the thresholds are universal. They depend on organism, tissue, and library prep. The point is to know what normal looks like for your setup, so that abnormal stands out.

1. Raw reads

Quality that drops early in the read. A gradual quality decline toward the 3′ end is normal for Illumina. A sharp drop in the middle of the read, or spikes of N calls across many samples, points to an instrument or run problem, not a biology problem. Check whether it affects one lane or the whole run.

High adapter content. Adapters appear when the insert is shorter than the read length. A little is fine and trimming handles it. A lot usually means short fragments, which often means degraded RNA or over-fragmentation.

A shifted or bimodal GC distribution. Your samples should share a similar, roughly unimodal GC curve. A second peak suggests contamination, such as bacterial DNA, rRNA, or adapter dimers. Overrepresented sequences in FastQC often tell you which.

Duplication, read carefully. High duplication is expected in RNA-seq, because highly expressed genes generate many identical reads. It becomes a red flag when it comes with low input, low library complexity, and a flat expression profile. Tools like dupRadar separate expression-driven duplication from PCR artifacts. If the library has UMIs, deduplicating on them makes the distinction much clearer.

2. Alignment and quantification

Low unique mapping rate. For a well-annotated organism such as human or mouse, a uniquely mapped rate well below your usual range is the first thing to investigate. Common causes are the wrong reference or annotation version, contamination, or a large rRNA fraction. Take a sample of the unmapped reads and check what they actually are, for example with FastQ Screen or a quick BLAST.

Strandedness mismatch. This one is quiet and costly. If a stranded library is counted as unstranded, or with the wrong strand setting, counts can drop by roughly half and antisense signal leaks into your genes. Confirm the library type instead of assuming it:

  • STAR’s ReadsPerGene.out.tab reports counts for all three strand settings side by side
  • Salmon with -l A infers the library type and records it in lib_format_counts.json
  • RSeQC’s infer_experiment.py reports the fraction of reads on each strand

High intronic or intergenic reads. In a poly(A) library, a large intronic fraction suggests genomic DNA contamination or a lot of unspliced pre-mRNA. In total RNA or single-nucleus data, a higher intronic fraction is expected. Context decides whether this is a problem.

3′ bias in gene body coverage. In poly(A)-selected libraries, coverage piling up at the 3′ end is a classic sign of RNA degradation. Compare it against RIN values if you have them. In 3′-tag methods such as QuantSeq or BRB-seq, the same pattern is the design, not a defect.

rRNA and mitochondrial fractions. High rRNA means depletion or poly(A) selection did not work well. Mitochondrial reads vary by tissue: heart and muscle are naturally high. What matters is a sample that is far above its own group.

3. Sample level

Library sizes that differ by an order of magnitude. Normalization handles moderate differences. Very uneven depth reduces power for the shallow samples, and can make them look like outliers for technical reasons alone.

PCA that separates by batch instead of biology. Color the PCA by every metadata column you have: prep date, lane, RIN, library size, operator. If PC1 lines up with prep date rather than condition, the batch effect is stronger than your biology. If batch and condition are perfectly confounded, no statistical correction can separate them.

One sample that does not belong. A sample-to-sample correlation heatmap makes outliers obvious. Before removing one, find out why it is different, and record the decision.

Sex genes that disagree with the metadata. Plot XIST against Y-linked genes such as RPS4Y1, DDX3Y, and KDM5D. A sample labeled female that expresses Y genes is a sample swap until proven otherwise. This takes five minutes and catches mistakes that no other QC step will.

Marker genes that point the wrong way. If your knockdown target is not reduced, or a treatment-response gene moves in the opposite direction, stop there. One caveat: in some knockout designs the transcript is still produced but missing the deleted exons, so check exon-level coverage before concluding the knockout failed.

4. Differential expression results

The p-value histogram. Under a well-behaved test, it should be roughly flat, with a spike near zero for the genes that are actually changing. A hump in the middle or a strong slope suggests the model does not fit the data, often because of unaccounted batch structure or overdispersion. A spike near 1 often comes from low-count genes and largely disappears with independent filtering. This plot takes one line of code and is the most underused diagnostic in RNA-seq.

Thousands of DE genes from a mild treatment. Sometimes the biology really is that strong. More often it is a batch effect aligned with the condition, or a global shift in composition that the normalization assumption does not handle.

Zero DE genes. Before concluding there is no effect, check power (two replicates per group is very little), the design formula, and the contrast. Then check the contrast direction again.

Top hits dominated by mitochondrial, ribosomal, or hemoglobin genes. These usually reflect sample quality or tissue composition, such as blood contamination, rather than the treatment.

Huge fold changes on low-count genes. A gene going from 1 to 8 reads is a 3-log2 fold change with almost no information behind it. Use fold-change shrinkage, such as lfcShrink with apeglm in DESeq2, before ranking genes or sending results for enrichment.

An asymmetric volcano plot. A strongly lopsided volcano can be real, but it can also mean a global expression shift that violates the assumption that most genes do not change. Check the MA plot and the normalization factors before interpreting it.

A short checklist

StageCheckQuick tool
Raw readsQuality, adapters, GC, overrepresented sequencesFastQC, MultiQC
AlignmentUnique mapping, strandedness, read distribution, gene body coverageSTAR logs, Salmon, RSeQC, Qualimap
SamplesLibrary size, PCA by metadata, correlation heatmap, sex genesDESeq2 vst + PCA, heatmap
DEp-value histogram, MA plot, fold-change shrinkage, contrast directionDESeq2, edgeR

A QC report does not tell you that a dataset is good. It tells you where to look. The red flags above are not reasons to discard data. They are questions that need an answer before the results go into a figure, a report, or a decision.
