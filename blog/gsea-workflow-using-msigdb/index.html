From Differential Expression to Biological Signatures: A Practical GSEA Workflow Using MSigDB

Differential expression analysis is often the first major step in RNA-seq interpretation. It tells us which genes change between two biological conditions, such as treatment versus control, disease versus normal tissue, or responder versus non-responder samples. However, a list of differentially expressed genes is rarely sufficient on its own. Individual genes are noisy, context-dependent, and difficult to interpret without a broader biological framework.

This is where gene set enrichment analysis, or GSEA, becomes useful.

Instead of asking whether a small list of statistically significant genes is over-represented in a pathway, GSEA asks a more powerful question: are the genes belonging to a known biological pathway or signature systematically shifted toward the top or bottom of a genome-wide ranked gene list?

This distinction matters. Many real biological processes are not driven by a handful of extremely significant genes. They may involve coordinated but moderate changes across dozens or hundreds of genes. A threshold-based approach can miss these patterns. GSEA preserves the full ranked structure of the differential expression result and tests whether predefined gene sets show non-random enrichment across that ranking.

In this workflow, RNA-seq differential expression results are analyzed against several Molecular Signatures Database collections using the fgsea algorithm. The goal is to move from gene-level statistical output to interpretable biological programs, including Hallmark pathways, curated biological pathways, regulatory target signatures, and Gene Ontology terms.

Why GSEA Instead of Only Over-Representation Analysis?

Traditional over-representation analysis usually starts with a filtered gene list. For example, one might define significant genes as those with adjusted p-value below 0.05 and absolute log2 fold change above 1. Then the method asks whether certain pathways contain more genes from that list than expected by chance.

That approach is simple, but it has weaknesses.

First, it depends heavily on arbitrary thresholds. A gene with adjusted p-value 0.049 is included, while a gene with adjusted p-value 0.051 is excluded, even though they are practically indistinguishable. Second, it discards ranking information. Third, it tends to favor large, well-annotated pathways and can miss subtle but coordinated transcriptional programs.

GSEA avoids these problems by using all genes that pass basic quality filtering. Each gene is assigned a ranking statistic, and gene sets are tested for enrichment at the extremes of that ranked list. In this workflow, the ranking statistic is the stat column from the differential expression result, which is typically the Wald statistic from DESeq2.

A positive statistic indicates that a gene is shifted toward the upregulated side of the comparison. A negative statistic indicates that a gene is shifted toward the downregulated side. By sorting genes from the highest statistic to the lowest statistic, the workflow creates the ordered vector required for GSEA.

This is a strong choice because the Wald statistic incorporates both effect size and uncertainty. Ranking by log2 fold change alone can over-prioritize noisy genes with unstable estimates, especially low-count genes. Ranking by p-value alone loses directionality. The signed test statistic is usually more appropriate for GSEA because it preserves both magnitude and direction of the differential signal.

Input Data Structure

The workflow expects a differential expression table containing at least the following columns:

  • gene: gene symbol used as the gene identifier
  • stat: signed differential expression test statistic
  • log2FoldChange: estimated expression change between conditions
  • padj: multiple-testing adjusted p-value

The script reads the input table from a tab-delimited text file and performs basic filtering to remove genes with missing values in critical columns. This is necessary because GSEA requires a clean named numeric vector. Missing gene names or missing statistics will cause incorrect rankings or failed enrichment tests.

After loading the data, the script prints the total number of genes, column names, and top upregulated and downregulated genes based on log2 fold change. This is not just cosmetic. It is a basic sanity check. Before running pathway analysis, one should verify that the differential expression result behaves as expected. If known marker genes are going in the wrong direction, or if the number of retained genes is unexpectedly low, pathway results should not be trusted.

Building the Ranked Gene List

The central object in the analysis is the ranked gene list:

gene_rank <- res_df$stat
names(gene_rank) <- res_df$gene
gene_rank <- sort(gene_rank, decreasing = TRUE)

This creates a named numeric vector where each value is the differential expression statistic and each name is the corresponding gene symbol. The vector is sorted from most positive to most negative.

Genes at the top of the list are most strongly associated with the test condition. Genes at the bottom are most strongly associated with the control or opposite condition, depending on how the original contrast was defined.

This step is critical because the interpretation of all downstream GSEA results depends on the direction of the contrast. If the differential expression comparison is test_vs_control, then positive normalized enrichment scores usually indicate pathways enriched in the test condition, while negative scores indicate pathways enriched in the control condition. If the contrast is reversed, the biological interpretation is reversed.

A common mistake is to interpret positive and negative enrichment scores without checking the original contrast design. That is not a minor labeling issue. It can completely invert the biological conclusion.

Loading MSigDB Gene Sets

The workflow uses msigdbr to access MSigDB gene sets directly from R. MSigDB is organized into collections, each representing a different type of biological knowledge.

This script uses four major collections:

Hallmark Collection

The Hallmark collection contains compact, well-curated gene sets representing major biological processes. These include pathways such as epithelial-mesenchymal transition, interferon response, oxidative phosphorylation, apoptosis, angiogenesis, hypoxia, and cell cycle programs.

Hallmark gene sets are usually the best first-pass interpretation layer because they reduce redundancy. Instead of returning hundreds of overlapping Gene Ontology terms, Hallmark analysis often gives a cleaner view of dominant biological programs.

C2 Curated Pathways

The C2 collection contains curated pathway gene sets from resources such as KEGG, Reactome, BioCarta, and other manually curated sources. These pathways are more granular than Hallmark signatures and can provide mechanistic detail.

C2 results are useful when the goal is to identify known signaling pathways, metabolic pathways, immune pathways, or disease-associated programs. However, they can also be redundant. Multiple pathway databases may describe similar biology with slightly different gene membership.

C3 Regulatory Target Signatures

The C3 collection captures regulatory signatures, including transcription factor targets and microRNA target gene sets. This layer helps move from pathway-level interpretation to possible upstream regulatory mechanisms.

For example, if a group of genes targeted by a particular transcription factor is enriched at the top of the ranked list, that may suggest activation of a regulatory program associated with that factor. However, this should be interpreted carefully. GSEA of target sets does not prove transcription factor activity directly. It generates hypotheses that should be checked against expression of the regulator, chromatin data, motif enrichment, perturbation experiments, or prior biological knowledge.

C5 Gene Ontology Collection

The C5 collection contains Gene Ontology terms, including biological process, molecular function, and cellular component annotations. GO is broad and useful, but also highly redundant. Many GO terms overlap heavily, and significant results often cluster around the same biological theme.

GO-based GSEA is useful for broad discovery, but it requires careful summarization. Reporting a long table of significant GO terms is weak analysis. The better approach is to group redundant terms into higher-level biological themes and then connect them back to the experimental design.

Converting MSigDB Data for fgsea

The msigdbr package returns gene set data in a tidy table format. Each row usually represents one gene-gene set membership relationship. The fgsea package requires a different format: a named list where each element is a gene set and each value is the vector of genes in that set.

The workflow performs this conversion by grouping the MSigDB table by gene set name and collapsing gene symbols into lists:

gene_sets <- msigdb_data %>%
  group_by(gs_name) %>%
  summarize(genes = list(gene_symbol), .groups = "drop") %>%
  deframe()

Conceptually, this transforms the data from a long table into a pathway-to-genes mapping.

One implementation detail should be corrected before production use: deframe() comes from the tibble package. The script should either load library(tibble) or call it explicitly as tibble::deframe(). Without that, the workflow can fail with the error:

Error in deframe(.) : could not find function "deframe"

A more robust version would be:

gene_sets <- msigdb_data %>%
  dplyr::group_by(gs_name) %>%
  dplyr::summarize(genes = list(gene_symbol), .groups = "drop") %>%
  tibble::deframe()

That small change makes the dependency explicit and improves reproducibility.

Running GSEA with fgsea

The workflow runs GSEA separately for Hallmark, C2, C3, and C5 gene sets using the fgsea() function.

The key parameters are:

fgsea(
  pathways = hallmark_sets,
  stats = gene_rank,
  minSize = 5,
  maxSize = 500,
  eps = 0
)

The pathways argument provides the MSigDB gene sets in list format. The stats argument provides the ranked gene vector. The minSize and maxSize arguments restrict the size of tested gene sets. Very small gene sets can be unstable, while very large gene sets can be too broad to interpret. In this script, gene sets smaller than 5 genes or larger than 500 genes are excluded.

The eps = 0 parameter requests more accurate p-value estimation rather than stopping at a lower bound. This is useful when highly significant enrichments are expected, although it may increase runtime.

Each GSEA result contains several important fields:

  • pathway: the name of the enriched gene set
  • pval: nominal p-value
  • padj: multiple-testing adjusted p-value
  • ES: enrichment score
  • NES: normalized enrichment score
  • size: number of genes from the gene set used in the test
  • leadingEdge: core genes contributing most strongly to the enrichment

The normalized enrichment score, or NES, is usually the most interpretable effect-size-like metric. A positive NES means the gene set is enriched toward the top of the ranked list. A negative NES means it is enriched toward the bottom.

The adjusted p-value controls for testing many gene sets. In this workflow, gene sets with padj < 0.05 are labeled significant.

Visualization Strategy

The script generates several plots to make the enrichment results interpretable.

Hallmark Results - Bar Plot

The Hallmark bar plot shows significantly enriched signatures ordered by NES. This is useful for quickly identifying whether the dominant transcriptional programs are mostly upregulated, mostly downregulated, or mixed. Red bars indicate upregulated pathways (positive NES), while blue bars indicate downregulated pathways (negative NES).

Hallmark Results - Significance Plot

The Hallmark scatter plot displays NES against -log10(adjusted p-value). This separates effect direction from statistical evidence. Pathways far from zero on the NES axis have stronger directional enrichment. Pathways higher on the y-axis have stronger statistical support. Dot size represents the number of genes in each pathway.

C2 Curated Pathways - Top Results

The C2 bar plot shows the top significant curated pathways. These are useful for deeper mechanistic interpretation after the broader Hallmark patterns are understood.

C3 Regulatory Targets - Top Results

The C3 bar plot reveals enriched transcription factor and miRNA target signatures, suggesting upstream regulatory mechanisms driving the observed gene expression changes.

Hallmark - Enrichment Score Curve

This classic GSEA enrichment plot shows the running enrichment score for the top significant Hallmark pathway. The curve represents the cumulative enrichment score across the ranked gene list. Blue bars at the bottom show where genes in this pathway appear in the ranking. A strong dip indicates genes are concentrated in one direction (either upregulated or downregulated).

However, visualization should not replace interpretation. A plot showing many significant pathways is not enough. The analysis must explain whether the enriched signatures make biological sense given the model system, treatment, tissue, disease state, batch structure, and experimental design.

Interpreting Positive and Negative Enrichment

Interpretation depends on the original contrast. Suppose the differential expression result is from test_vs_control.

In that case:

  • Positive NES means the pathway is enriched among genes upregulated in the test condition.
  • Negative NES means the pathway is enriched among genes downregulated in the test condition, or relatively higher in the control condition.

This distinction is important. GSEA does not say that a pathway is universally activated or suppressed in an absolute sense. It says that the gene set is directionally enriched relative to the ranked contrast provided.

For example, if an interferon response signature has a positive NES in treated_vs_control, the interpretation is that interferon-response genes are shifted toward the treated condition. If the same analysis were run as control_vs_treated, the NES would likely flip direction.

Therefore, a good report should always state the contrast clearly.

Exported Results

The workflow exports result tables for each collection:

  • GSEA_MSigDB_Hallmark_Results.csv
  • GSEA_MSigDB_C2_CuratedPathways_Results.csv
  • GSEA_MSigDB_C3_RegulatoryTargets_Results.csv
  • GSEA_MSigDB_C5_GeneOntology_Results.csv
  • GSEA_MSigDB_Summary.csv

Each table includes the pathway name, NES, p-value, adjusted p-value, enrichment score, gene set size, and direction.

This is useful because different audiences need different levels of detail. A high-level report may focus on Hallmark results, while a mechanistic follow-up may require C2, C3, and leading-edge genes.

The summary file counts the number of significant gene sets in each collection and separates them into upregulated and downregulated categories. This gives a quick overview of the global enrichment landscape.

Why Leading Edge Genes Matter

One of the most important parts of GSEA interpretation is the leading edge. The leading edge genes are the subset of genes that contribute most strongly to the enrichment score.

Two pathways can have similar NES values but be driven by very different genes. Conversely, many enriched pathways may appear distinct but share the same leading edge genes. Without inspecting the leading edge, pathway interpretation can become superficial.

For serious biological interpretation, the next step after this workflow should be leading-edge analysis. This includes identifying recurrent genes across top pathways, checking whether they are biologically coherent, and determining whether they are known markers, regulators, or downstream effectors.

For example, if multiple immune-related pathways are significant and share the same leading-edge genes, the result may reflect a single dominant immune infiltration or inflammatory program rather than multiple independent mechanisms.

Quality Control and Common Failure Modes

GSEA results are only as good as the ranked list and annotation quality. Several issues can compromise the analysis.

The first issue is incorrect gene identifiers. This workflow uses human gene symbols from the gene column and MSigDB human gene symbols. If the expression table uses Ensembl IDs, outdated symbols, mouse genes, mixed identifiers, or duplicated gene names, the overlap with MSigDB may be poor or misleading.

The second issue is duplicate gene symbols. RNA-seq tables can contain multiple rows mapping to the same symbol, especially when transcript-level or Ensembl-derived annotations are collapsed incorrectly. GSEA expects one statistic per gene. Duplicate symbols should be resolved deliberately, for example by keeping the row with the strongest absolute statistic or using a documented aggregation strategy.

The third issue is contrast direction. If the contrast is mislabeled, all upregulated and downregulated interpretations become inverted.

The fourth issue is confounding. If batch, sex, donor, sequencing run, tissue composition, or other covariates are not modeled correctly in the differential expression step, GSEA may enrich technical artifacts instead of biology.

The fifth issue is pathway redundancy. C2 and C5 collections can produce many overlapping significant terms. Reporting every significant term without collapsing themes is not publication-quality interpretation.

The sixth issue is annotation version drift. MSigDB, organism annotations, and gene symbols change over time. A reproducible workflow should record package versions, MSigDB version, organism database version, R version, and the exact input contrast.

What This Workflow Does Well

This workflow has several strong design choices.

It uses all genes rather than only significant genes, which is appropriate for GSEA. It ranks genes by the signed statistical test statistic, preserving direction and uncertainty. It analyzes multiple MSigDB collections, allowing interpretation at different biological levels. It exports both tables and plots, making the results suitable for downstream review. It also summarizes significant pathways by collection and direction, which helps compare broad enrichment structure across databases.

The workflow is especially useful as a structured first-pass interpretation layer after RNA-seq differential expression analysis.

What Should Be Improved Before Production Use

For a more reproducible and production-grade analysis, several changes are recommended.

Package installation should not occur inside the analysis script every time it runs. Installing packages during analysis makes execution slower, less reproducible, and dependent on the current state of external repositories. A better approach is to use renv, a Docker image, Conda environment, or workflow-managed container.

The script should record package versions and MSigDB metadata. This is important because pathway definitions can change across database releases.

The workflow should explicitly check for duplicate gene names before creating the ranked vector. It should also report the number of genes overlapping each MSigDB collection.

The output should include leading-edge genes, not only pathway-level summaries. Leading-edge genes are essential for serious biological interpretation.

The analysis should also include session information, input file checksum, contrast metadata, sample metadata summary, and parameters used for GSEA.

Finally, the plots should handle the case where no pathways are significant. As written, plotting after filtering to padj < 0.05 may fail or generate empty figures if no gene sets pass the threshold.

Biological Interpretation Framework

A rigorous interpretation should follow this order:

  1. Confirm that the differential expression contrast is correct.
  2. Inspect the Hallmark results to identify broad biological programs.
  3. Use C2 curated pathways to refine mechanistic interpretation.
  4. Use C3 regulatory signatures to generate hypotheses about upstream transcriptional or post-transcriptional regulators.
  5. Use C5 Gene Ontology results to support broader biological themes, while avoiding over-interpretation of redundant terms.
  6. Inspect leading-edge genes across top pathways.
  7. Connect the enrichment results back to the experimental system, known biology, phenotype, and sample metadata.

This prevents the common mistake of treating pathway names as conclusions. A pathway enrichment result is not a mechanism by itself. It is evidence that a defined set of genes is non-randomly distributed in the ranked differential expression profile. Mechanistic claims require additional support.

Conclusion

This GSEA-MSigDB workflow provides a practical bridge between RNA-seq differential expression statistics and biological interpretation. By ranking all genes using the signed differential expression statistic and testing curated MSigDB gene sets with fgsea, the analysis can detect coordinated transcriptional programs that may be missed by threshold-based gene list methods.

The most interpretable starting point is the Hallmark collection, followed by deeper exploration of curated pathways, regulatory target signatures, and Gene Ontology terms. The exported tables and visualizations provide a useful foundation for reporting, but the strongest biological conclusions should come from careful inspection of contrast direction, leading-edge genes, pathway redundancy, metadata, and experimental design.

Used correctly, this workflow can help transform a flat differential expression table into a structured biological narrative. Used carelessly, it can produce attractive plots with weak or misleading interpretation. The difference depends on disciplined ranking, annotation control, reproducible execution, and biologically grounded review.
