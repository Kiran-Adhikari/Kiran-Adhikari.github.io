About Me

Hi, I’m Kiran Adhikari, a bioinformatics scientist with a background in pharmaceutical sciences. I work at the intersection of biology, computational science, and medicine, using genomic data to investigate disease mechanisms, discover biomarkers, and explore potential therapeutic targets.

My work ranges from analyzing RNA-seq and single-cell datasets to developing bioinformatics pipelines that process millions of sequencing reads. I’m particularly interested in understanding what molecular data can tell us about disease, and just as importantly, what it cannot.

A little about my background

My academic journey started in pharmacy. I completed my B.Pharm in Pharmacy and M.Pharm in Pharmaceutical Technology at Gujarat Technological University, India. My master’s research focused on developing a sustained-release formulation of favipiravir, an antiviral drug.

Before moving into bioinformatics, I worked at Accuprec Research Laboratories, an OECD GLP contract research organization, where I was involved in preclinical toxicology studies using animal models and cell lines. Working alongside pharmacologists, toxicologists, microbiologists, and veterinarians gave me firsthand experience with experimental research and drug safety evaluation.

During this work, I became interested in the molecular changes behind biological responses. Knowing whether a compound was toxic was important, but understanding which genes, pathways, and cellular processes were affected seemed equally valuable. This interest eventually brought me to bioinformatics.

I completed my M.S. in Bioinformatics at Hood College, Maryland, where I worked with Dr. Miranda Darby and Dr. Daniel Sierra-Sosa on splice-site prediction using RNA-seq data.

We developed splice-junction datasets from publicly available sequencing data and compared different machine-learning approaches. Interestingly, our neural network did not outperform a simpler linear model. It was a good lesson in why benchmarking matters and why more complex models are not always better. A manuscript from this work is currently in preparation.

What I do

Most of my work involves applying computational methods to biological questions, often in collaboration with researchers from different scientific backgrounds.

At AmpSeq, I work with academic and government laboratories on projects involving transcriptomics, genomics, toxicology, infectious diseases, radiation biology, and stem-cell research.

My role goes beyond running analyses. I work with investigators on experimental design, data quality, statistical approaches, and biological interpretation. I also develop and maintain bioinformatics pipelines for large-scale sequencing datasets, using technologies such as Nextflow, Argo, Docker, Kubernetes, and HPC infrastructure.

Some days involve exploring gene-expression patterns or comparing cell populations. Others involve troubleshooting sequencing workflows, optimizing computational resources, or figuring out why a dataset behaves differently than expected. That variety is one of the things I like about bioinformatics.

At Karyon Bio, my research focuses on metabolic liver disease, liver fibrosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma.

I analyzed bulk and single-cell transcriptomic datasets to identify disease-associated gene signatures and investigate changes in liver cell populations. Much of this work involved comparing findings across independent patient cohorts to understand which molecular patterns were reproducible.

I also worked on machine-learning approaches for biomarker discovery and clinical prediction, including co-developing a decision-support tool to help identify patients who may benefit from additional liver fibrosis imaging.

This research contributed to two first-author conference presentations in 2026 at the Saudi Liver Meeting and ICGM. I also contributed to an NIH grant proposal focused on AI-based biomarker discovery in metabolic liver disease.

Earlier, at Cian Diagnostics, I worked on long-read sequencing, variant calling, and DNA methylation analysis, including research involving portable DNA sequencing technologies.

What interests me

I’m interested in the connection between molecular data and disease biology.

Transcriptomics and other high-throughput technologies allow us to study thousands of genes and complex cellular systems, but finding a statistically significant result is only one part of understanding a disease. The more interesting questions often come afterward: Is the finding reproducible? Does it reflect a meaningful biological mechanism? Can it help identify a therapeutic target?

My interests extend to molecular pharmacology, neuroscience, and drug discovery, particularly understanding the molecular mechanisms of neurological and psychiatric disorders.

Having worked in both pharmaceutical research and computational biology, I would like to bring these areas closer together by combining computational findings with experimental approaches to target discovery and validation.
